Wat Plai Laem is a modern temple complex on a lake about 1 km east of Big Buddha, built between 2004 and 2009 in a bright Thai-Chinese style. It is best known for its white 18-armed Guanyin and a large laughing Buddha (Phra Sangkachai), each on its own platform in the water. Entry is free, the grounds are open roughly 06:00–18:00, and fish food from the machines costs a few baht. Allow 30–45 minutes, and cover shoulders and knees.

Map and directions

Wat Plai Laem sits on a lane off Route 4171, the road that loops around the north-east tip between Bang Rak (Big Buddha) and Choeng Mon. Coming from Big Buddha, drive east for about 1 km and look for the temple gate on the inland side. The Guanyin is tall and white, so you see it from the lane.

Get directions: open Wat Plai Laem in Google Maps. Some map listings show late-night opening hours; ignore them. The temple grounds close around 18:00.

Thai and Chinese Buddhism side by side

Wat Plai Laem looks unlike most Thai temples because it mixes two Buddhist traditions on one site. Thailand follows Theravada Buddhism, with the ordination hall (ubosot), monks in saffron robes and seated Buddha images. Samui's Chinese-descended families, many with Hainanese roots, also venerate figures from Mahayana Buddhism and Chinese folk religion, above all Guanyin, the bodhisattva of compassion. Wat Plai Laem puts both in the same lake: a Thai ordination hall in the middle, a Chinese-style Guanyin on one side and a big-bellied monk on the other.

The complex is modern. It was built between 2004 and 2009, partly to give the north-east of the island a showpiece temple alongside Big Buddha. Some visitors find the colours loud; others see it as a confident modern version of Thai-Chinese temple art. Either way, it is an active temple. Locals come to pray, make merit and have their fortunes told, and monks live on the grounds.

The temple's formal name is Wat Laem Suwannaram, though almost everyone calls it Wat Plai Laem after the area.

Who is Guanyin? The story behind the statue

Guanyin began as Avalokiteshvara, the Indian bodhisattva of compassion: an enlightened being who chooses to stay in the world to help others. As the figure travelled through China over many centuries, Guanyin came to be shown mostly as a woman in white robes, and became one of the most loved figures in East Asian Buddhism. Her name means "the one who hears the cries of the world".

Many-armed forms are an old way of showing that compassion: more arms, more ways to help. The best known is the "thousand-armed" Guanyin; 18-armed versions like the one at Wat Plai Laem are less common. The objects in her hands vary from statue to statue and can include a lotus, a vase of pure water, a rosary, a wheel and a sword.

In Thailand, Chao Mae Kuan Im is popular well beyond the Chinese community. Thai and Thai-Chinese worshippers ask her for health, safe travel, children and success in business. Devotees often avoid beef, and during the annual vegetarian festival in the ninth Chinese lunar month many eat no meat at all. On Koh Samui you can see her at Wat Plai Laem, at the Chinese Temple of Guanyin in Taling Ngam on the west coast, and in small shrines in Maenam and Nathon, where the island's Hainanese families keep their temples. Guanyin shrines are busiest at Chinese New Year (Saturday 6 February 2027). For the wider picture, see Thai culture and traditions.

What you'll see, step by step

  1. 1. The gate and the lake. From the car park you walk out onto paths and bridges across a shallow lake. Catfish, carp and turtles crowd the edges whenever someone holds out food.
  2. 2. The 18-armed Guanyin. The white statue stands on its own platform in the water. Each of the 18 hands holds an object or makes a gesture, a way of showing a compassion with many means of helping people; a small Buddha image sits in the crown. In Thailand she is called Chao Mae Kuan Im. Many Thai-Chinese visitors avoid eating beef because of their devotion to her, and you will see offerings of fruit and flowers rather than meat.
  3. 3. The ordination hall. The ubosot appears to float on the lake between the two big statues. Its outside is covered in ceramic tile and its walls hold murals of Buddhist scenes. Take your shoes off before you step inside and sit with your feet pointing away from the Buddha image.
  4. 4. Phra Sangkachai, the laughing Buddha. Across the water sits a large, round, smiling figure. In Thai tradition this is Phra Sangkachai, a disciple of the Buddha known for his wisdom; he is often confused with Budai, the Chinese "Happy Buddha", who looks similar. Both are linked with good fortune.
  5. 5. Merit-making corners. Look for the stick-shaking fortune ritual (siam si): you kneel, shake a cylinder of numbered sticks until one falls out, and read the matching slip. You can also sign a ceramic tile in return for a donation; the tiles go into temple building work.
  6. 6. Smaller shrines. Around the edges are smaller Buddha images, Chinese deities and guardian figures. Walk slowly; the details are the point of this temple.

Feeding the fish

Feeding the fish and turtles counts as merit-making. Use only the food sold by the temple: there are small dispensers and stalls by the halls, and a bag costs a few baht. Don't throw bread, crisps or anything else, which clouds the water and harms the fish. Children love it, but keep them back from the edges of the platforms; the water is shallow but the drops are sudden and some railings are low.

Wat Plai Laem dress code and etiquette

  • Cover shoulders and knees. Bring a light scarf or sarong; some Samui temples lend wraps and some don't.
  • Shoes off before entering the ordination hall or any room with a Buddha image.
  • Don't climb on statues or platforms, and don't sit or pose with your feet towards a Buddha image or Guanyin.
  • Women should not touch monks or hand them items directly.
  • Photos: outdoor photos are fine. Inside the hall, follow any signs, switch off the flash and keep the shutter sound off during prayers.
  • Drones: don't fly them over the temple without permission.

Best time to visit and photo tips

Come at about 08:00. Tour vans that combine Big Buddha and Wat Plai Laem arrive from about 09:30 and the narrow paths get crowded. Morning light falls on the front of the Guanyin and the lake is often still enough for reflections. Midday is hot, with little shade over the water. Late afternoon is pleasant too, but the grounds close around 18:00.

  • Best angle for the Guanyin: from the bridge facing the platform, with the lake in the foreground.
  • Reflections: early morning on windless days.
  • Season: February to April is the driest. October to December is the north-east monsoon; November is the wettest month (about 445 mm). Showers are heavy but often short, so wait under the hall roof.

Combine with Big Buddha and the north-east

Almost everyone pairs Wat Plai Laem with Big Buddha, about 1 km west. The road between them has no pavement and steady traffic, so drive or take a Grab rather than walking. A relaxed half day in the north-east:

  1. 07:30 Big Buddha before the crowds (free, 07:00–18:30)
  2. 08:30 Wat Plai Laem and the fish
  3. 09:30 Swim at Choeng Mon, one of the calmest bays, about 3 km east
  4. 12:00 Lunch at Plai Laem or on Bang Rak beach
  5. Evening Fisherman's Village, especially on Friday for the walking street (about 17:00–23:00)

Parking, facilities and accessibility

  • Parking: cars and scooters park inside the temple grounds at no charge on normal days. [check]
  • Facilities: basic toilets, drinks stalls and small shops selling offerings near the halls.
  • Accessibility: most of the site is flat paving and low bridges, manageable with a pushchair; the platforms and the hall have a few steps. There is little shade.
  • Safety: wet tiles are slippery after rain. Keep a hand on small children near the water.

Seasonal and safety notes

Wat Plai Laem is open all year. In the monsoon months (October to December) roads in the north-east can flood briefly after cloudbursts, and the lake paths get slippery. In the hottest months (April–May, highs around 32–33 °C) visit early and carry water. The temple is close to the airport, so it is a good last stop on the way to a flight; allow at least 15 minutes from the temple to check-in.
