From October to December, the north-east monsoon brings big surf, rip currents and red flags to Chaweng and Lamai on the east coast: don't swim when the red flag is up. For a box jellyfish sting, pour vinegar over it for at least 30 seconds, don't rub it, don't use fresh water, and call 1669. The north and west coast beaches (Maenam, Bang Por, Lipa Noi, Taling Ngam) are tidal and very shallow at low tide, so swim at high tide and wear water shoes.

Red flags are most common on the east coast from October to December, when the north-east monsoon pushes onshore wind and surf onto Chaweng and Lamai. They also fly during winter cold surges. On 19 February 2026, for example, strong winds sent 1–2 m waves onto Chaweng and Lamai, red flags went up along both beaches and swimming was banned. Drownings on Samui have followed swimmers ignoring red flags. The UK government's travel advice says simply: follow all warning signs, especially red flags.

The rest of the year, from January to September, the east coast is usually calm, and February to April are the calmest months of all. See our Koh Samui weather guide and sea conditions page for the month-by-month picture.

Lifeguards

Lifeguard cover on Samui is limited. Some busy stretches of Chaweng and Lamai have lifeguard posts in the high season, but most Samui beaches, including the quiet coves and the whole west coast, have no lifeguard at all. [check]

Hotel pool staff are not beach lifeguards. Assume that nobody is watching, swim with a friend, and tell someone on shore when you go in. If you see someone struggling, call 1669 and the hotel, and throw a float rather than swimming out yourself.

Official weather warnings

The Thai Meteorological Department issues marine warnings when the north-east monsoon or a cold surge strengthens, typically advising small boats to stay ashore and warning of waves of 2 m or more. When these warnings are in force, expect red flags on the east coast and cancelled boat trips. Check tmd.go.th or the wave forecast on Windy before planning a beach day.

Rip currents: how to spot and escape one

A rip is a narrow channel of water flowing away from the beach. It can carry even strong swimmers out faster than they can swim back. On Samui, rips form on the east coast when waves are breaking, especially near rocks and headlands at either end of Chaweng and Lamai.

How to spot a rip

  • A gap in the breaking waves where the water looks calmer and darker.
  • Foam, seaweed or sand-coloured water moving out to sea.
  • Choppy, rippled water in a line running away from the shore.

If you are caught

  1. Don't panic and don't swim straight back against the current.
  2. Float on your back, keep your head up and raise one arm to signal for help.
  3. Swim parallel to the beach until you are out of the current, then swim back at an angle.
  4. If someone else is in trouble, don't swim out. Shout for help, throw something that floats (a body board, cool box or life ring) and call 1669.

Box jellyfish and other stingers

Box jellyfish are the most dangerous animals in Samui's sea. Of the 10 deaths from box jellyfish recorded in Thailand since 2002, 9 were in waters off Samui and Koh Phangan. After deaths in the 2010s, hotels on both islands were given multilingual warning signs and vinegar stations, and marine officials post warnings when sightings are reported.

  • When the risk is higher: in the rainy months (the UK government says May to October nationally), after heavy rain, in calm, warm water, in the evening and at night.
  • Where: any beach can be affected. Swim inside netted or buoyed areas where they exist, and ask your hotel about recent sightings.
  • Protection: a full-length rash vest or stinger suit protects most of the body.

First aid for a box jellyfish sting

  1. Get the person out of the water and keep them still.
  2. Pour vinegar (4–6% acetic acid) over the sting for at least 30 seconds.
  3. Don't rub it, don't rinse with fresh water, don't apply ice.
  4. Call 1669. Severe pain, confusion, chest pain, breathing problems or skin colour changes mean hospital immediately.
  5. If the person stops breathing, start CPR until help arrives.

Smaller jellyfish and sea lice cause itchy rashes rather than emergencies; vinegar or seawater and an antihistamine help. Our Koh Samui hospitals guide lists the nearest emergency departments.

Tides, rocks and coral

Tidal beaches on the north and west coasts

Samui's north and west coasts face sheltered, shallow water. At low tide, Bang Por, Lipa Noi, Taling Ngam and Bang Kao turn into wide flats of sand, rock and coral rubble, and you may have to walk 50 m or more to reach swimming depth. Swim around high tide, wear water shoes and shuffle your feet to avoid stingrays. Check the times in our Koh Samui tides guide.

Rocky coves

Coral Cove, Silver Beach and Chaweng Noi are small coves with boulders and coarse sand. They are good for snorkelling in calm weather, but wear water shoes: sea urchins live in rock crevices, and coral cuts infect easily. Don't stand on coral; it injures you and kills the reef.

Lad Koh and other headlands

The rocks below Lad Koh viewpoint, between Chaweng Noi and Coral Cove, take the full force of monsoon waves. In November 2024 a tourist was swept off these rocks by a large wave. Stay on the viewing platform when the sea is rough, and never climb down to the water's edge for photos in the October–December season. The same applies to Hin Ta and Hin Yai at the south end of Lamai.

Boats, jet skis and swimmers

Chaweng and Lamai have jet skis, banana boats and parasailing in front of the main hotels, and longtails come in close to pick up passengers. Accidents between boats and swimmers happen in Thailand every year.

  • Swim inside buoyed swimming zones where they exist, and stay close to shore elsewhere.
  • Snorkellers should use a brightly coloured buoy or swim near the boat with a guide watching.
  • Jet-ski riders must keep clear of swimmers and slow down near the beach. If you rent one, read our scams and jet ski safety guide first.
  • Never swim near piers where ferries and speedboats come and go.

On our boat trips, crews brief you before every snorkel stop, life jackets are available for every age and size, and we cancel when the sea isn't safe.

Sun, heat and hydration

Samui sits at about 9° north. The UV index is high all year, even through cloud, and sea temperatures of 28–30 °C mean you stay in the water longer than you think.

  • Use reef-safe mineral sunscreen; chemical filters such as oxybenzone are banned in national parks, including Ang Thong.
  • Wear a rash vest and hat when snorkelling; backs and the backs of legs burn first.
  • Drink water before you are thirsty and add rehydration salts on hot days.
  • Don't swim after heavy drinking, and never swim alone at night.
  • Leave valuables in the hotel safe. Phones and wallets left on the sand while you swim are the most common beach theft on Samui, and a waterproof pouch costs less than a replacement passport.
  • Watch for heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, dizziness and heavy sweating. Get into the shade, cool down with water and drink; confusion or fainting needs a doctor.

Night swimming, alcohol and party beaches

Many drownings in Thailand involve alcohol, darkness or both. Beach bars on Chaweng and Lamai stay open late, and a midnight swim after a few drinks is exactly when currents, jellyfish and boats are hardest to see. Box jellyfish are also more likely to come close to shore at night. If you go in after dark, stay in the shallows, never alone, and never after drinking. The same applies on Full Moon nights on Koh Phangan.

Children at the beach

  • Choose calm beaches: Choeng Mon, Lipa Noi at high tide, and Chaweng's north end in the dry months.
  • Keep children within arm's reach in the water. Drowning is fast and silent.
  • Put a rash vest and water shoes on small children; it covers most sting and coral risks.
  • Pack vinegar in the beach bag, and teach older children not to touch jellyfish, even dead ones on the sand.
  • Avoid the east coast entirely when red flags are up from October to December.
