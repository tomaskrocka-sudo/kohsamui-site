Cycling on Koh Samui works best on the flatter coastal stretches, such as the back lanes of Maenam and Bophut and the quiet west coast between Lipa Noi and Nathon, and early in the morning before the heat and traffic build. The 51 km ring road is busy with trucks, minivans and scooters, and the interior climbs to 635 m at Khao Pom on very steep concrete, so most visitors are happier on an e-bike or on short local rides than on a full loop. Bicycle hire is a small business on Samui: a handful of shops and many resorts keep bikes, so ask your hotel first.

Good to know: cycling is a niche on Samui, and rental shops change often. Prices and routes on this page are a guide, last reviewed in November 2026; check the bike and the price in person.

If you ride regularly at home and are comfortable in traffic, you will enjoy early-morning rides here. If you only cycle occasionally, keep to short rides near your hotel or join a guided outing.

Where to rent and what it costs

There is no big bike-hire scene on Samui as there is in Chiang Mai. Your options:

  • Your hotel or resort: many beach resorts in Maenam, Bophut, Choeng Mon and Lipa Noi lend simple bicycles to guests, often free or for a small fee.
  • Bike and sports shops: a few shops in Chaweng, Bophut and Lamai sell and rent mountain bikes, road bikes and e-bikes; some also service bikes. [check]
  • Scooter rental shops: a few keep electric bikes or electric mopeds alongside scooters. [check]
  • Bring your own: some long-stayers fly in with their bike or buy one on the island and sell it on when they leave.

Before you ride away

  • Test both brakes properly; salt air corrodes cables quickly.
  • Check the tyres for cracks and the chain for rust.
  • Ask for a helmet, a lock and lights, and a phone number for breakdowns.
  • Photograph the bike with the owner present, as you would a scooter.
  • Agree the deposit in writing and never leave your passport.

E-bikes, e-scooters and the law

  • Electric kick scooters (the stand-up type) can't be registered as vehicles in Thailand, so riding them on public roads isn't legal. [check]
  • Electric mopeds (seated, no pedals or token pedals) are motorcycles in law: they need registration plates, compulsory insurance, a helmet and a motorcycle licence with an International Driving Permit, exactly like a petrol scooter.
  • Pedal-assist e-bikes are widely ridden like ordinary bicycles, but Thailand has no specific e-bike category, so their legal position is unclear. [check]
  • Helmets: the motorcycle helmet law, with fines up to 2,000 THB per person since June 2025, applies to anything classed as a motorcycle. Wear a cycling helmet on any bike; Samui's roads give you every reason to.
  • Insurance: check your travel policy. Cycling is usually covered as a leisure activity, but an electric moped counts as a motorcycle, with the same licence and IDP conditions.

The rules for scooters, licences and checkpoints are on our scooter rental guide and roads guide.

The best routes

Flat coastal rides

  • Maenam beach lanes: the small roads behind Maenam beach run past guesthouses, coconut palms and the Chinese temple, with short side trips to the sand. Easy and largely traffic-free.
  • Bophut to Bang Rak: from Fisherman's Village east towards the Big Buddha, using side lanes where you can; finish with a coffee by the sea.
  • The west coast, Lipa Noi to Nathon: the quietest flat stretch on the island, best in the late afternoon heading north into the sunset at Lipa Noi. Carry lights for the ride home.
  • Bang Por and the north-west: quiet fishing-village roads and seafood restaurants on the sea.

These routes follow the flattest parts of the coast; traffic levels on the connecting ring-road sections change by time of day. [check]

Climbs

  • The Na Muang road: from the ring road up to Na Muang Waterfall and beyond; a steady climb with a cooling swim at the top.
  • The Lamai hills: the roads above Maret towards Overlap Stone are short but brutally steep concrete; walk the steepest parts.
  • The Lamai–Chaweng hill on the ring road: a fast, curving road with heavy traffic; only for confident riders at first light.
  • Route 4170 to Taling Ngam: short, sharp climbs with blind bends in the south-west.

Mountain bikers will find dirt tracks through the coconut and rubber plantations of the interior, but many are on private land, and some become impassable mud in the rainy season. Can you cycle around the whole island?

Yes, but it is a serious ride rather than a leisurely one. The ring road loop is 51 km, plus detours to beaches. A fit cyclist on a road or hybrid bike can ride it in about 3–4 hours of riding time, more with stops and the climbs between Lamai and Chaweng and in the south-west. Start at first light, ride clockwise so the sea and the beach stops are on your left, carry 2 litres of water and refill at 7-Eleven stores, and plan a long lunch stop rather than riding through the midday heat. On an e-bike, check the battery range first; the hills drain it quickly.

Safety

  1. Ride early: start at dawn and finish by about 10:00, before the heat and the traffic peak.
  2. Lights: sunset is between about 18:00 and 18:45 depending on the month, and dusk is short in the tropics. Front and rear lights are essential.
  3. Keep left and be seen: wear bright colours; drivers don't expect bicycles.
  4. Watch turning traffic: cars and scooters pull out of side roads and make U-turns without looking.
  5. Rain: wet concrete and painted road markings are slippery, and the first minutes of a downpour are the worst. Shelter under a shop awning and wait.
  6. Potholes and sand: especially on bends near beaches and building sites, and from October to December.
  7. Dogs: if a dog chases you, slow down rather than sprint, put the bike between you and the dog, and don't kick out.
  8. Water and sun: drink before you're thirsty, and use sun cream on arms, neck and legs.
  9. Phone and numbers: carry a phone with 1669 (ambulance) and 1155 (Tourist Police) saved.
  10. Insurance: make sure your travel policy covers cycling; see travel insurance.

What to bring on a ride

  • At least 1.5–2 litres of water, plus cash for refills and fruit stalls.
  • Sun cream, sunglasses and a cap under the helmet.
  • A small repair kit and pump; bike shops are few and far between outside Chaweng and Bophut.
  • A light rain jacket or a dry bag for your phone from October to December.
  • Front and rear lights, even on a morning ride.
