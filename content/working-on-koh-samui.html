To work for a business on Koh Samui you need two things: a non-immigrant visa (usually the Non-B) and a work permit, both sponsored by a Thai employer that meets the legal requirements. Working on a visa exemption or a tourist visa is illegal, and so is working for a Thai employer or Thai clients on a Destination Thailand Visa, which only covers remote work for foreign employers and clients. Some occupations are reserved for Thai nationals altogether, including tour guiding, Thai massage, hairdressing and driving, and a foreigner caught working illegally faces a fine of 5,000–50,000 THB and deportation.

Not legal advice: this page summarises the rules as of November 2026 for general information. Work permit and visa rules change and are applied with some discretion; check your situation with a licensed Thai lawyer or the Department of Employment before you start work.

How work permits work

Foreign employment is governed by the Foreign Workers Management Emergency Decree of 2017, as amended in 2018, and administered by the Department of Employment under the Ministry of Labour. Thai law defines work broadly: it means using your physical effort or knowledge, whether or not you are paid. [check]

What the employer needs

Under the standard rules, the sponsoring business must have:

  • Capital: generally 2 million THB of paid-up registered capital for each foreign employee.
  • Thai staff: at least 4 Thai employees, registered for social security, for each foreign work permit.
  • A real job: a position that isn't on the reserved list, with a salary that meets immigration's minimums for your nationality.

Companies promoted by the Board of Investment (BOI), representative and regional offices, and some other employers may have these requirements modified or waived. Minimum monthly salaries for a Non-B extension vary by nationality and are set by immigration. [check]

Steps and timing

  1. Before you travel The employer prepares company documents; you apply for a Non-Immigrant B visa at a Thai embassy or by e-visa.
  2. Arrival Enter Thailand on the Non-B visa (90 days).
  3. First weeks The employer files the work permit application, now mostly through the online e-WorkPermit system, with your passport, photos, degree certificates, a medical certificate and the job details. Processing typically takes 3–7 working days.
  4. Start work Only once the permit is issued, and only in the job, location and employer stated on it.
  5. Extension Before your 90 days run out, apply at Koh Samui Immigration in Maenam for a one-year extension of stay tied to the job.

Government work permit fees are about 3,000 THB per year, in line with the LTR digital work permit fee published by the BOI; agents and lawyers charge on top. [check] Changing employer, job title or workplace means updating the permit; leaving the job cancels it.

Visa extensions, 90-day reports and re-entry permits are all handled at Koh Samui Immigration; see the immigration office guide.

Jobs reserved for Thais

The Ministry of Labour lists 40 occupations that are prohibited or restricted for foreigners: 27 completely closed, 3 open only under international agreements, 8 open only with an employer and 2 open only with employer approval and entry under a government agreement. Accountants, engineers and architects can work only under agreements with specific countries, and construction, carpentry, agriculture and some other skilled trades only with an employer and conditions. Whether a particular job counts as "agent" or "guide" work can be a grey area; get advice before you accept it.

Even with a permit, you may only do the job described on it, for the employer named on it. A dive instructor with a permit can teach diving but can't, for example, also sell villas on the side.

Remote work, DTV and LTR

The Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) is the route most remote workers on Samui now use. It is valid for 5 years, allows stays of up to 180 days per entry, costs 10,000 THB (or the local equivalent) and needs proof of at least 500,000 THB in funds. Since 31 August 2026, embassies require proof of permanent residence in the country where you apply and a criminal record clearance certificate.

  • Allowed on a DTV: working remotely for an employer outside Thailand, or freelancing for clients outside Thailand.
  • Not allowed on a DTV: a Thai work permit, working for a Thai company, or freelance work for Thai clients.

The Long-Term Resident (LTR) visa's work-from-Thailand professional category is for employees of large foreign companies with an income of at least USD 80,000 a year (or USD 40,000–80,000 with a master's degree); it doesn't come with a work permit because the work is for a foreign employer. Other LTR categories can work for Thai entities with a digital work permit. Our guide for digital nomads on Koh Samui covers coworking, internet and costs, and the Thailand visa guide compares every visa.

Staying 180 days or more in a calendar year makes you a Thai tax resident, and foreign income brought into Thailand can then be taxable; take advice from a tax adviser.

Volunteering and "free" work

Because Thai law counts work whether or not you are paid, volunteering, working in exchange for accommodation, helping at a friend's bar or doing a "work exchange" at a hostel or retreat can all count as working without a permit. Registered volunteer programmes with charities can arrange the right visa and a permit; informal arrangements usually can't. If someone offers you free lodging for help, assume you need a permit unless a lawyer tells you otherwise.

Deportation usually comes with a blacklisting that makes it hard to return to Thailand. Enforcement on Samui is real: a multi-agency raid on hillside villas in July 2025 also examined nominee ownership and undocumented workers.

Starting a business on Samui

Many foreigners on the island run their own villa, restaurant, dive or wellness business. Most service businesses fall under the Foreign Business Act, which limits foreign shareholding to under 50% unless you have a Foreign Business Licence, BOI promotion or a treaty exemption such as the US Treaty of Amity. Using Thai nominee shareholders to get round this is illegal. Take advice from a licensed lawyer before you sign a lease or invest. Checklist before you accept a job on Samui

  1. Is the job on the reserved list? If it involves guiding, massage, driving, selling at markets or acting as an agent, stop and take advice.
  2. Will the employer get you a Non-B visa and a work permit before you start, and pay the fees?
  3. Does the business have the capital and the Thai staff to sponsor you? Ask to see the company registration.
  4. Is the job title on the permit the job you will actually do?
  5. Does your salary meet immigration's minimum for your nationality, so your yearly extension will be approved?
  6. Is the employer registering you for Thai social security and withholding income tax?
  7. Who files your TM.30 address notification and 90-day reports?
  8. What happens to your visa if the job ends? Your extension is usually cancelled with the permit, and you may have only a short time to leave or change status.

If an employer suggests you "start now and sort the paperwork later", or work on a tourist stamp "like everyone else", walk away: it is you who is fined and deported. For broader safety and legal pitfalls, see our Koh Samui safety guide.
