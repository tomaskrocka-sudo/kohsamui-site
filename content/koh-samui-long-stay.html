Most long-stayers on Koh Samui rent a condo, house or villa in Maenam, Bophut, Lamai or the hills above Chaweng Noi on a 6- or 12-month contract. Studios and one-bedroom condos run from about 12,000 THB a month in Maenam to 30,000 THB in Chaweng, and private pool villas start at around 40,000 THB; our long-term villas are monthly rent on request, minimum 6 months. Before you sign, check how electricity is billed (the landlord's rate per unit can be far above the state tariff), what the deposit covers, and that the landlord will file your TM.30 address notification.

Good to know: rents and bills here are approximate ranges checked in late 2026 and change with season and demand. Contract and visa points are general information, not legal advice.

Visas for long stays

Since 15 September 2026, most Western nationalities get 30 days visa-free, extendable once by up to 30 days at Koh Samui Immigration in Maenam. That is fine for a first month, not for a long stay. The usual long-stay routes are:

Contracts and deposits

  • Length: 6 and 12 months are standard; shorter stays usually cost more per month.
  • Deposit: landlords commonly ask for a security deposit of one to two months' rent plus the first month in advance. [check]
  • Written contract: get a contract in English and Thai, with the rent, deposit, what is included, notice period and who pays for repairs.
  • Inventory and photos: photograph and video every room, appliance and meter reading on move-in day, and have both sides sign the inventory.
  • Notice and early exit: agree in writing what happens if you leave early; many contracts keep the deposit if you break a fixed term.
  • Owner: check you are dealing with the owner or an authorised manager, and ask to see the title deed or the condo's ownership document if you are paying several months up front.
  • Payment: pay by bank transfer and keep receipts. Never pay a large deposit to secure a place you haven't seen in person.
  • Getting the deposit back: agree the final meter readings and inspection date before you move out, and ask when and how the deposit will be returned.

Contract checklist

  • Names, passport and ID numbers of both parties
  • Address and description of the property, with the inventory attached
  • Rent, due date and bank details
  • Deposit amount and refund terms
  • Electricity and water rates per unit, or a statement that you pay the official bills
  • What is included: internet, cleaning, pool, garden, rubbish collection
  • Who pays for repairs and routine maintenance
  • Notice period and early-termination terms
  • The landlord's commitment to file the TM.30

Utilities and extras

  • Electricity: the biggest variable. Some landlords pass on the official bill from the Provincial Electricity Authority (PEA); others charge their own rate per unit, often well above the state tariff. Air-conditioning running day and night in a villa can easily use several hundred units a month. Read the meter on move-in and ask to see recent bills.
  • Water: usually cheap, but some areas rely on private suppliers or delivered water in the dry season (February–April). Ask whether the house has a tank and pump, and how water is billed.
  • Internet: condos often include fibre; houses may need their own plan. AIS 3BB Fibre3, True Online and NT sell home plans from about 100 Mbps to 1 Gbps. See SIM cards and Wi-Fi.
  • Cleaning, pool and garden: villas often include pool and garden care once or twice a week; extra cleaning is usually paid per visit.
  • Drinking water: buy bottled water or a refill service; don't drink the tap water.
  • Gas: kitchens usually run on bottled gas, delivered by local shops.

The PEA residential tariff works out at roughly 4 THB per unit before VAT, while some landlords charge 6–8 THB or more per unit. [check]

TM.30 and registering your address

Thai law requires the owner, householder or manager of a place where a foreigner stays to notify immigration within 24 hours on form TM.30. On a long rental that means your landlord or condo manager, not you, though you are the one who suffers if it isn't done: extensions and 90-day reports are often refused without a matching TM.30 receipt.

  • Ask the landlord to file the TM.30 when you move in, and keep a photo of the receipt.
  • Owners can file online at tm30.immigration.go.th after registering once.
  • Late or missing notifications can be fined up to 2,000 THB.
  • If you move, the new address needs a new TM.30.
  • Staying more than 90 days in a row also means a 90-day report (TM.47).

Full details: Koh Samui immigration office.

Viewing a rental: what to check

  • Internet: run a speed test on the Wi-Fi in the room where you will work, and check mobile signal for both AIS and True.
  • Air-conditioning: switch on every unit; ask when they were last cleaned. Dirty units use more power and smell in the rainy season.
  • Water: run the shower and the kitchen tap together to check the pressure; ask about the tank, pump and hot water.
  • Damp and leaks: look for stains on ceilings and black mould in wardrobes and bathrooms, the signs of a leaking roof or poor ventilation.
  • Insects and animals: fly screens on windows, sealed drains and no gaps under doors make a big difference to ants, mosquitoes and geckos.
  • Noise: visit in the evening as well as the day; bars, temples, roosters, building sites and the airport flight path all affect sleep.
  • Access: drive the road in a car and on a scooter; steep concrete and unlit lanes are harder at night and in rain.
  • Security: working locks, a safe for passports, and lighting outside.
  • Kitchen and laundry: a real oven is rare; check the fridge size and whether there is a washing machine.
  • Neighbours: ask whether short-stay guests use neighbouring villas; holiday groups can be noisy.

Daily life on a long stay

  • Shopping: big supermarkets and malls in Chaweng and Bophut stock imported food; fresh markets in Lamai, Maenam and Nathon are cheaper for fruit, vegetables and fish.
  • Money: foreign cards pay 220–250 THB per ATM withdrawal, so a wise long-stayer withdraws larger amounts less often or uses a transfer service. Opening a Thai bank account usually needs a long-stay visa and a proof of address. See banks and money.
  • Healthcare: private hospitals in Chaweng and Bophut and the government hospital near Nathon cover most needs; see hospitals and dentists.
  • Community: the island's long-stay community is spread out; gyms, yoga studios, Muay Thai camps and coworking spaces are the easiest places to meet people.
  • Rubbish and recycling: collection varies by area; ask the landlord which days rubbish is collected and where to leave it.

Seasons and availability

  • Hardest to find: December–April and July–August, when holiday demand is highest and some owners prefer short holiday lets. Start looking 1–2 months ahead.
  • Easiest and cheapest: May–June and September–November. October to December is the rainy season, so it is also the time to see which roads flood and which houses leak.
  • Rainy season checks: from October to December, check the access road, drainage around the house and whether the roof leaks before you sign a year's contract.
  • A first month: many long-stayers book a holiday villa or hotel for the first 2–4 weeks and view long-term rentals in person.

Owners are often more flexible on rent for a 12-month contract signed in the low season than for a 6-month one starting in December. Weather month by month: Koh Samui weather.

Long stays with children

Families usually choose Maenam, Bophut or Choeng Mon for calm beaches and quieter roads. A few points that matter more with children:

  • Schools: Samui has international and bilingual schools, mostly on the north and east of the island; visit and compare fees, curriculum and the school run before choosing where to live. [check]
  • Pools: many villa pools are unfenced; ask whether a fence or alarm can be fitted.
  • Transport: a car is safer than scooters for school runs; ask rental firms for child seats.
  • Health: Bangkok Hospital Samui has a paediatrics department; keep vaccinations up to date and use repellent against dengue.
  • Visas: children under 20 can join a parent's DTV as dependants, and children of work-permit holders can usually get dependant visas; check the rules for your family's situation with the embassy before you travel.
