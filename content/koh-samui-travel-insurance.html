Thailand doesn't require travel insurance for visa-exempt tourists, but you should treat it as essential: hospitals in Thailand ask for an insurer's guarantee of payment or a deposit before they treat you, and private care on Samui is expensive. The clause that fails most often is the motorcycle one: without a home licence and an International Driving Permit that both show the motorcycle category, most scooter claims are refused. If we cancel one of our boat trips for weather, you choose a full refund or a new date, so you won't need to claim for that.

Good to know: this page explains what to look for; it is general information, not financial or legal advice, and it doesn't recommend any insurer. Policy wording varies widely, so read the exclusions in your own policy.

Is travel insurance mandatory for Thailand?

Not for ordinary tourists. Visa-exempt visitors (30 days for most Western nationalities since 15 September 2026) don't have to show insurance at the border. Some long-stay visas are different:

Visa or statusHealth insurance required?
Visa exemption (30 days) and tourist visaNo, but strongly advised
Destination Thailand Visa (DTV)Not on the standard embassy document list; check your embassy's checklist
Long-Term Resident (LTR)Yes: health cover of at least USD 50,000, or Thai social security, or USD 100,000 held in a bank for 12 months
Non-Immigrant O-A (retirement, applied for abroad)Yes: Thai embassies require health insurance that meets a set minimum for the whole stay

Scooters and motorbikes

Riding a scooter is where most visitors' insurance quietly stops working. Check these conditions before you hire one:

  • Licence: you normally need a full motorcycle licence at home. A car licence does not cover a scooter, even a 110 cc one.
  • IDP: Thailand expects visitors to carry an International Driving Permit with the motorcycle category alongside the home licence. The UK government warns that driving without a valid licence could invalidate your travel insurance. You can only get an IDP in your home country.
  • Helmet: riding without one is illegal and is a common exclusion.
  • Engine size: many policies cap cover at 125 cc, and the UK government advises checking that your policy covers the vehicle type and engine size. Many rental scooters on Samui are 150–160 cc.
  • Registered bike: the UK government says many rental bikes in tourist areas are unregistered and can't legally be used on a public road; check the plate, tax disc and registration book.
  • Damage to the bike: travel policies rarely cover damage to a hired vehicle. That falls on you and the shop's deposit.

Diving, snorkelling and the recompression chamber

Many visitors learn to dive or do fun dives from Samui, with trips to Sail Rock, Ang Thong and Koh Tao. Standard policies often cover diving only to a set depth and only within your certification.

  • Check the depth limit (often 18 m for beginners and 30 m for advanced divers) and whether you must dive with a qualified instructor or buddy.
  • Check that decompression illness and chamber treatment are covered; they are expensive. Some divers take a separate dive-specific policy.
  • Snorkelling is usually covered as a normal holiday activity.

Jet skis and water sports

Many standard policies exclude jet skis or cover your injuries but not your liability for damage to the craft. On Samui the bigger risk is often financial: damage claims by beach jet-ski operators are a long-running problem. Photograph the jet ski before you ride, never leave your passport as a deposit, and call the Tourist Police on 1155 if a dispute starts. A guided trip with written terms is a safer way to ride: our Jet Ski Safari to Pig Island costs 9,950 THB per jet ski.

Hospitals and paying

The UK government notes that all hospitals in Thailand require a guarantee of payment before treating patients. On Samui that works in one of two ways:

  • Guarantee or direct billing: show your policy and your insurer's 24-hour assistance number at admission. The hospital contacts the insurer, which issues a guarantee of payment. Some insurers have direct-billing agreements with Samui's private hospitals; ask at the desk.
  • Pay and claim: for outpatient visits, or if the insurer hasn't confirmed cover, you pay by card or cash and claim later. Expect a deposit before admission or surgery.

Keep every receipt, the medical report (ask for it in English), prescriptions and a discharge summary. Call your insurer's emergency line before any planned admission if you can; some refuse claims for non-urgent treatment they didn't approve.   • Ang Thong Marine Park closes every year from 1 November to 15 December and reopens on 16 December. Because the closure is scheduled, it isn't an insurable surprise; plan around it. Pig Island runs all year.

Day tours are fully refundable 48 hours or more before departure, 50% at 24–48 hours and not refundable under 24 hours or for a no-show; Ang Thong tours are fully refundable 48 hours or more before departure and not refundable after that.   • Ferries and flights: ferries to Koh Phangan and Koh Tao can stop in strong north-east winds, mostly from October to January. Check your policy's travel-disruption and missed-departure sections, and leave a buffer day before international flights.
  • Storms: once a storm is named or forecast, policies bought afterwards usually exclude it. Buy insurance when you book the trip.

Theft and making a claim

  1. Hour 1 Block cards in your banking apps and lock or wipe a stolen phone.
  2. Day 1 Report the theft to the Tourist Police (1155) or the police station for the area, and ask for a written report in English or with an English summary. Most insurers want it within 24 hours.
  3. Day 1 Call your insurer's assistance line, especially for medical treatment or a lost passport.
  4. During your stay Keep receipts for replacement items, taxis to hospital or the police, and any extra nights.
  5. At home Submit the claim with the police report, medical report, receipts and proof of ownership.

Why claims from Samui get refused

  • Riding a scooter without a motorcycle licence and IDP, or above the policy's engine size.
  • Injuries after drinking, including falls on the way home from a bar.
  • No police report, or a report made days later.
  • Planned or non-urgent treatment that the insurer didn't approve first.
  • Undeclared pre-existing medical conditions.
  • Activities not on the policy's list, such as jet skis or diving beyond the depth limit.
  • Valuables left unattended on a beach or in a scooter basket.

Long stays, digital nomads and annual cover

Most single-trip holiday policies limit each trip to 30–90 days and require you to have a home address and healthcare system to return to. If you are staying for months on a DTV or a long-stay visa, look at an international health policy instead. Check whether it covers outpatient care, maternity, scooters and evacuation, and whether it pays Thai hospitals directly. A quick pre-departure checklist

  • Medical cover with a high limit and evacuation included.
  • Your activities listed: scooter (with engine size), diving (with depth), jet ski, trekking.
  • Your licence and IDP sorted if you plan to ride.
  • The insurer's 24-hour number saved in your phone and on paper.
  • A digital copy of the policy and your passport in your email.
  • Pre-existing conditions declared.
