Pharmacies are easy to find on the main roads of Chaweng, Lamai, Bophut, Maenam and Nathon, and inside the malls and big supermarkets, most open until about 21:00–22:00. Late at night, the safest option is a private hospital's emergency department, where a doctor can see you and the hospital pharmacy dispenses around the clock. Thai pharmacists can sell many medicines that need a prescription at home, but controlled medicines (strong painkillers, some ADHD and sleeping drugs) need a Thai FDA permit to bring into the country, and cannabis needs a Thai prescription.

Last checked: November 2026. Medicine import rules are set by the Thai Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and can change. If you take a controlled medicine, check with the Thai FDA or a Thai embassy at least a month before you travel.

How Thai pharmacies work

  • Pharmacist on duty: a full pharmacy (look for the green or blue cross and a Thai pharmacy licence on the wall) must have a licensed pharmacist present during opening hours. Smaller shops that only sell packaged household medicines don't need one.
  • What they can sell: Thai rules let pharmacists sell a wider range than in the UK, EU, US or Australia, including some medicines that are prescription-only at home. Controlled drugs (opioids, sedatives, stimulants) are not sold without a prescription, and reputable pharmacists will ask questions before selling anything stronger than everyday remedies.
  • Generic names: the same medicine has different brand names in Thailand. Show the pharmacist the generic name (for example "ibuprofen", "loratadine", "omeprazole") or a photo of your packaging.
  • Ask for the leaflet: packs are often split into strips or sold in small bags. Ask for the patient leaflet or ask the pharmacist to write the dose, timing and generic name on the bag.
  • Prices: local generics are cheap; imported brands cost more than at home. Prices are usually fixed in chain stores and can be higher in shops on Chaweng Beach Road.

Hospitals and pharmacies generally accept foreign prescriptions as a guide, but a Thai pharmacist is not obliged to dispense a medicine just because a foreign doctor prescribed it.

Late-night and 24-hour options

Most street pharmacies close between 21:00 and 23:00. A few in central Chaweng stay open later in high season, but opening hours change often, so don't rely on one. After hours:

  1. For anything more than a headache, go to a private hospital emergency department (all have 24-hour emergency rooms). A doctor will see you and the hospital pharmacy will dispense.
  2. For minor needs (paracetamol, plasters, rehydration salts), try 7-Eleven, which is open 24 hours in most areas.
  3. Ask your hotel reception: many keep a basic first-aid supply and know which pharmacy is open.

Bringing your own medication

Thailand controls the import of medicines, and the rules are stricter than many visitors expect.

  • Everyday medicines (blood pressure tablets, contraceptive pills, asthma inhalers, insulin): bring enough for your trip in the original packaging, with a copy of your prescription or a doctor's letter listing generic names and doses.
  • Quantity: the general limit for personal use is a supply of up to 30 days. [check]
  • Controlled medicines (narcotic and psychotropic medicines, such as strong opioid painkillers, some ADHD stimulants, some sleeping tablets and benzodiazepines): you may need a permit from the Thai FDA before you travel, or a medical certificate in a specific form. Apply well in advance.
  • Carry it in hand luggage, keep the pharmacy labels, and declare at customs if unsure.
  • Cannabis-based medicines from home (including medical cannabis and many CBD oils): don't bring them. Thai rules on cannabis extracts are strict and differ from those at home.

Some medicines legal at home are restricted or banned in Thailand. The safest route is to email the Thai embassy in your country with the generic names of your medicines a month before you travel. See our Thailand visa guide for other things to sort out before you fly.

Holiday essentials to buy or bring

  • Seasickness tablets: dimenhydrinate is sold in most pharmacies. Take it 30–60 minutes before the boat leaves, not once you feel sick. Some types cause drowsiness, so don't drive afterwards. Ginger sweets help some people.
  • Oral rehydration salts: for heat, hangovers and stomach bugs. Sold in sachets in every pharmacy and many 7-Elevens.
  • Insect repellent with DEET or picaridin: dengue mosquitoes bite in the daytime.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen: Thailand's national parks, including Ang Thong National Marine Park, ban sunscreens containing oxybenzone, octinoxate, 4-methylbenzylidene camphor or butylparaben. Mineral (zinc oxide) sunscreens are the simplest choice, and a rash vest is better still. Park rangers can check bags, and the rule protects the reefs you come to see on our Ang Thong Marine Park and snorkelling trips.
  • Vinegar for box jellyfish stings. Many hotels have vinegar stations; a small bottle in the beach bag costs a few baht. Our beach safety guide explains the first aid.
  • Waterproof plasters and antiseptic for coral cuts and scooter grazes, which infect quickly in the heat.
  • Antihistamine tablets and cream for bites, sea lice and minor stings.
  • After-sun and aloe vera gel: the sun at 9° north burns fast, even on cloudy days.

Antibiotics and prescription medicines

Many Thai pharmacies will sell common antibiotics on request. That doesn't make it a good idea. Most holiday coughs, sore throats and stomach bugs are viral, and the wrong antibiotic won't help, can cause side effects, and adds to antibiotic resistance, which is a serious problem in Southeast Asia. See a doctor at a clinic or hospital first: a consultation is quick, and you'll get the right drug at the right dose, with a receipt for your insurer.

Fake and substandard medicines exist. Buy from a licensed pharmacy with a pharmacist on duty, check that packs are sealed and in date, and never buy medicine from street stalls, beach sellers or social media accounts. Be especially wary of cheap "brand-name" erectile dysfunction pills, slimming products and painkillers.

Cannabis and CBD products

Since 25 June 2025, cannabis flower can only be sold with a prescription from a licensed doctor, dentist, pharmacist or Thai traditional medicine practitioner, and each prescription is limited to a 30-day supply. Smoking cannabis in public is banned, with fines of up to 25,000 THB. The Thai Cabinet approved a stricter draft Cannabis Control Act in September 2026, which is now before parliament. Extracts with more than 0.2% THC remain narcotics. CBD products sold in shops must be approved by the Thai FDA; many products on sale are not.

Never take cannabis or cannabis products out of Thailand: you can be arrested at the airport or in a transit country. Full details are on our drugs, vapes and alcohol law page.

Boat-trip first-aid checklist

Before a day on the water, pack:

  • Seasickness tablets (taken before departure).
  • Reef-safe sunscreen, a hat, sunglasses and a rash vest.
  • Water (at least 1.5 litres per person) and rehydration salts.
  • Your regular medicines in a waterproof bag, plus asthma inhaler or adrenaline pen if prescribed.
  • Waterproof plasters and antiseptic wipes.
  • Water shoes for rocky landings.
