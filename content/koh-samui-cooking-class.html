Dishes you'll only find on Samui and in the south

Southern Thai food is hotter, sourer and more turmeric-yellow than the dishes most visitors know. Ask your cook for one of these if the class menu allows it:

  • Wai khua: octopus cooked in coconut milk, Samui's signature dish.
  • Gaeng som or gaeng lueang: a thin, fiery sour curry coloured by fresh turmeric, usually with fish.
  • Gaeng tai pla: a pungent curry made with fermented fish innards; an acquired taste.
  • Khua kling: dry-fried minced pork or beef with a lot of curry paste and lime leaves.
  • Pad sataw goong: stir-fried prawns with bitter "stink beans".
  • Khanom jeen: fresh rice noodles with southern curries and a basket of raw vegetables.

The market visit

Many classes start at a fresh market, where the cook shows you the herbs and pastes you'll use: three kinds of basil, galangal versus ginger, kaffir lime, pea aubergines, fresh turmeric and the curry-paste stalls. On our class it is an optional stop on the way. If you want to explore on your own, the Lamai fresh market opens from about 06:00 to 20:00 and is best before 09:00; the Hua Thanon fish market is a morning market on the south-east coast; see Hua Thanon. Bring small notes if you want to buy spices or a mortar to take home.

Diets and allergies

  • Vegetarian and vegan: Thai cooking adapts easily: soy sauce replaces fish sauce, tofu and mushrooms replace meat. The hidden ingredient is shrimp paste in many curry pastes, so ask for a paste made without it. Tell us when you book.
  • Gluten: soy sauce and oyster sauce usually contain wheat. Gluten-free soy sauce can be used if requested in advance.
  • Nuts: peanuts appear in pad thai, som tam and massaman; cashews in stir-fries. Easy to leave out if you warn the cook.
  • Shellfish: shrimp paste, dried shrimp and oyster sauce are everywhere. A severe allergy needs a clean station and a cook who understands it; tell us in writing.
  • Chilli: you add your own, so you can cook any dish from "no spice" to "Thai hot".

Classes for children and families

Children who can stand at a counter and follow instructions usually enjoy a class, especially the pounding of curry paste and rolling spring rolls. Gas burners and sharp knives mean an adult should cook alongside younger children. Tell us the children's ages when you enquire; we confirm the minimum age with the kitchen and can book a private family session so the pace suits them. Choose the morning class with younger children: it ends with lunch, before the afternoon slump.

How to choose a school

  • Own station or shared? Hands-on means you cook every dish yourself, not watch a demonstration.
  • Group size: 4–6 guests is ideal; a long table of a dozen means less help from the cook.
  • Transfer: check it is included from your area and whether there is a surcharge.
  • Menu choice: can you pick your dishes, and are southern dishes on offer?
  • Market or garden: a market visit adds an hour; a herb garden is quicker and cooler.
  • Recipes: a printed booklet or digital file you can use at home.
  • Time of day: morning for lunch, afternoon or evening for dinner.

A rainy-day plan

From October to December Samui gets most of its rain, often in heavy afternoon bursts. A morning cooking class fills the wettest part of a grey day, and an afternoon at a spa or a Muay Thai class rounds it off: see Muay Thai on Koh Samui. When the weather clears, the sheltered south-west coast is the best bet for a boat trip: the Pig Island speedboat tour (1,450 THB) runs all year from Thong Krut. More ideas in Koh Samui in November. Learning longer: cooking courses and the DTV

If you want weeks rather than hours in a Thai kitchen, Thailand's Destination Thailand Visa (DTV) has a "soft power" track that covers Thai cooking courses as well as Muay Thai. It allows stays of up to 180 days per entry and needs a course enrolment and proof of funds. Read our Thailand visa guide before you book a long course.
