Wat Ratchathammaram, better known as Wat Sila Ngu or the Red Temple, stands on the ring road between Lamai and Hua Thanon in the south-east of Koh Samui. Its main hall is terracotta red from roof to floor and covered in carved reliefs of guardians, sea creatures and figures from Buddhist and Hindu stories. Entry is free, it is open 07:00–17:00, and you need about 30 minutes. Go in the morning, when the low light brings out the carving.

Map and directions

The temple sits on the seaward side of Route 4169 at the southern end of Lamai, between the turn for Hin Ta and Hin Yai and the village of Hua Thanon. The red roofline is easy to spot from the road. Its chedi, Phra That Sila Ngu, stands about 50 m away within the grounds.

Get directions: open the Red Temple in Google Maps. On some maps it appears under its Thai name or as Wat Phra That Sila Ngu.

The names: Ratchathammaram, Sila Ngu and "Red Temple"

The temple's official name is Wat Ratchathammaram. Locals usually call it Wat Sila Ngu, after its chedi: in Thai, sila means stone or rock and ngu means snake, so the name translates roughly as "snake rock". We have not found a reliable written account of where the name comes from, so treat any story you hear on the spot as local tradition. Visitors and drivers simply call it the Red Temple, because of the colour of the main hall.

A modern temple in an old style

The red hall is a recent building rather than an ancient one, and visitors over the last decade have watched work continue on parts of the site. Its look is unusual for Samui: one deep brick-red colour and carved relief everywhere, instead of the white-and-gold walls and glass mosaic of most island temples. That is what makes it worth a stop: nowhere else on the island has a whole hall worked in relief like this.

It is a working temple. Monks live on the grounds, local people come to make merit, and there is a cemetery with urns of ashes on the lower slope towards the sea. Visit quietly.

What you'll see, step by step

  1. 1. The entrance guardians. A giant guardian figure and naga (serpent) balustrades guard the steps up to the main hall (viharn).
  2. 2. The outer walls. Walk slowly around the outside. Almost every surface is carved: sea monsters, guardian figures and characters from Buddhist mythology stacked along the walls and eaves. Look for Hindu figures that are common in Thai temple art, such as Ganesha and the monkey general Hanuman. Garudas, the bird-men who are the royal emblem of Thailand, sit along the roof.
  3. 3. Inside the hall. Take your shoes off. The interior walls continue the red relief work, and a Buddha image sits at the far end. Sit with your feet tucked away from it if you stay a moment.
  4. 4. The chedi and bodhi tree. About 50 m away is the chedi, Phra That Sila Ngu, with a Buddha image inside, and a bodhi tree, the species under which the Buddha attained enlightenment.
  5. 5. The sea view. Paths lead down the slope towards the shore, where a viewing deck looks out over the coast south of Lamai.

Good to know: there is little shade anywhere on the site. Bring a hat, water and sunglasses, especially between 11:00 and 15:00.

Red Temple etiquette and dress code

  • Cover shoulders and knees before you enter any building. Bring a sarong; not every Samui temple lends wraps.
  • Shoes off inside the hall and any room with a Buddha image.
  • Don't touch or lean on the reliefs. Carved surfaces wear quickly, and climbing on them for photos is disrespectful.
  • Photos: exterior photos are fine. Inside, follow any signs and don't use flash during prayers.
  • The cemetery: keep to the paths and don't photograph mourners.
  • Women should not touch monks or hand them items directly.

Best time and photo tips

The temple opens at 07:00. The first two hours give the best light: low sun throws shadows across the carving and shows its depth, while flat midday light makes the red look dull. It is also quieter early, as visitor numbers rise from mid-morning, with peaks in July–August and December. By season: February to April is driest; October to December is the north-east monsoon, with November the wettest month (about 445 mm). Wet red stone looks rich in photos but the steps get slippery.

Parking and facilities

  • Parking: space for cars and scooters by the temple, off the ring road. Pull in and out carefully; Route 4169 is fast here.
  • Facilities: basic; bring your own water. Restaurants and shops are a few minutes away in Lamai and Hua Thanon.
  • Accessibility: the hall is up steps and the grounds slope to the sea, so wheelchair access is limited. The exterior carving can be seen from the level area at the front.

Is the Red Temple worth a stop?

Yes, if you are passing, and almost every route to the south of the island passes it. It is not a half-day sight like Big Buddha and Wat Plai Laem together, and some visitors find 10–15 minutes enough. Give it 30 minutes if you like art and detail: the carving rewards a slow walk around the walls, and the sea view from the lower grounds is a quiet extra that most tour groups skip. If you only have time for one temple in the south, choose Wat Khunaram for its story or the Red Temple for its look; with a scooter and a morning, you can do both plus Guan Yu in under three hours.

Seasonal and safety notes

The temple is open all year. In the monsoon months (October to December) sudden downpours make the steps and stone slippery and can flood stretches of the ring road for an hour or two. In April and May, when highs reach 32–33 °C, the stone gets hot underfoot; go early. The ring road outside is one of the busiest on the south-east coast, so take care crossing it and pulling out of the car park.
